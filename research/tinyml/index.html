TinyML for Embedded Systems — Techniques and Trade-offs

A survey and practical analysis of the primary model compression and optimisation techniques used to deploy machine learning models on resource-constrained embedded targets — evaluating each technique's impact on accuracy, model size, and inference latency on ARM Cortex-M and ESP32 hardware.

Abstract

The deployment of machine learning models on microcontrollers — a field known as TinyML — requires navigating fundamental constraints of RAM, Flash, and compute throughput that are orders of magnitude more restrictive than those encountered in cloud or mobile inference. This survey examines the four primary techniques used to bridge the gap between model capability and hardware constraint: post-training quantisation, weight pruning, knowledge distillation, and the use of hardware-efficient neural network architectures. Each technique is analysed for its impact on model accuracy, size, and inference latency, evaluated empirically on ARM Cortex-M4 (STM32F4) and ESP32 (Xtensa LX6) targets using the TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers and CMSIS-NN inference frameworks.

Introduction

The exponential growth of connected sensing devices and the parallel advance of neural network capabilities have created a compelling opportunity: intelligent inference at the very edge of the network, running directly on the microcontrollers already embedded in billions of devices worldwide.

TinyML — defined broadly as machine learning inference on devices with memory constraints below 1MB SRAM — has moved from a research curiosity to a practical engineering discipline in the space of a few years. Applications span keyword spotting, gesture recognition, anomaly detection, image classification, and predictive maintenance — all running without network connectivity, with latency measured in milliseconds and power consumption in microwatts.

Despite this progress, deploying a trained model onto an embedded target remains a non-trivial engineering challenge. This survey provides a systematic analysis of the techniques available to compress, optimise, and adapt neural networks for microcontroller deployment.

Key constraints for TinyML model deployment are:

  • Model size (Flash): The model weights must fit in Flash. For many targets this limits models to under 200–500 KB.
  • Runtime memory (SRAM): Intermediate activation tensors during inference consume SRAM. This is often the binding constraint, as SRAM is significantly smaller than Flash.
  • Compute throughput: MCUs without hardware floating-point units cannot efficiently compute FP32 tensor operations, making integer quantisation not just beneficial but essential.

Quantisation

Quantisation maps model weights and activations from higher-precision floating-point representations to lower-precision fixed-point integers. The most common target in TinyML is INT8 (8-bit integer), which is directly supported by CMSIS-NN SIMD instructions on ARM Cortex-M targets.

Post-Training Quantisation (PTQ)

PTQ converts a pre-trained FP32 model to INT8 without retraining. A representative calibration dataset of 100–500 samples is used to compute per-layer activation scaling factors. PTQ is fast and requires no modification to the training pipeline, making it the most practical approach for most TinyML projects.

Quantisation-Aware Training (QAT)

QAT simulates quantisation effects during the forward pass of training by inserting fake quantisation nodes. This allows the model to adapt its weights to the quantisation error, typically recovering 1–3% accuracy compared to PTQ. QAT is recommended for models where PTQ introduces unacceptable accuracy degradation.

Weight Pruning

Pruning removes weights from a trained model that contribute minimally to its output. Magnitude-based pruning zeros out weights below a threshold, and the resulting sparse model can be stored more compactly. Structured pruning removes entire filters or neurons, which directly reduces the computational graph without requiring sparse matrix support.

Practical Limitation for MCU Targets

Unstructured (weight-level) pruning produces sparse matrices that require sparse computation kernels to realise inference speed improvements. Most MCU inference frameworks, including TFLM and CMSIS-NN, use dense matrix operations and cannot benefit from unstructured sparsity. Structured pruning is therefore preferred for embedded targets.

Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation trains a small student model to mimic the output distribution of a larger, more accurate teacher model. Rather than training the student on hard class labels, it is trained on the teacher's soft probability outputs — which carry more information about inter-class relationships than binary labels.

Distillation is particularly effective when the student model is too small to converge effectively on complex tasks when trained directly. A ResNet-50 teacher model can transfer knowledge to a 5,000-parameter student CNN, improving accuracy by 3–5% compared to direct training on hard labels.

Efficient Architectures

Rather than compressing standard architectures, several model families are designed from the ground up for efficiency:

  • MobileNets: Use depthwise separable convolutions to reduce computation by 8–9× versus standard convolutions.
  • SqueezeNet: Uses 1×1 convolutions aggressively to reduce parameter count while maintaining competitive accuracy on ImageNet.
  • EfficientNet-Lite: Compound scaling of depth, width, and resolution, optimised specifically for integer quantisation.
  • DS-CNN: Depthwise Separable CNN designed specifically for keyword spotting on Cortex-M targets. Achieves 95% accuracy on Speech Commands with under 100KB footprint.

Conclusion

TinyML is a mature enough field to be applied to practical engineering problems — as demonstrated by the elephant detection work accompanying this survey. The most accessible path for most projects is: design an efficient architecture from the start, apply INT8 post-training quantisation, and deploy via TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers.

When accuracy requirements are stringent, quantisation-aware training recovers most of the accuracy lost in PTQ. Knowledge distillation is most valuable when target model capacity is severely constrained and direct training fails to converge adequately.

The most significant open challenge remains the SRAM constraint: while Flash can store large models after quantisation, SRAM is the practical binding constraint for most MCU targets. Research into operator scheduling, activation checkpointing, and model micro-segmentation will define the next frontier of TinyML capability.
