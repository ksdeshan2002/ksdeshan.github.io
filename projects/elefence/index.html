E-LeFence

An AI based elephant proximity alert system designed as an add-on to Sri Lanka's existing electric fence network — using motion sensing, AI-driven elephant identification, and fence integrity monitoring to warn villagers and authorities before an elephant reaches their doorstep.

Project Overview

Human-elephant conflict (HEC) has become one of Sri Lanka's most pressing wildlife conservation problems, causing significant human and elephant fatalities every year. Electric fences — more than 1,000km of them — are the primary mitigation strategy currently deployed, but elephants have learned to breach them by testing, pushing, or using logs and tusks to collapse fence posts. Once a fence is compromised, there is no real-time way to know a breach has happened until it is too late.

E-LeFence is a compact device designed to sit on top of the existing fence infrastructure rather than replace it. It combines a PIR motion trigger, an AI-based camera module that identifies elephants specifically (as opposed to other animals), a gyroscope-based fence tilt monitor, and a fence voltage sensing unit — all feeding into a communication pipeline that calls both villagers and the relevant authorities the moment a breach is confirmed.

Why not just rely on the electric shock?

Elephants are intelligent enough to test a fence with a light touch before committing, and some have learned to cross without making contact at all. Existing detection add-ons in the region — laser beams and geophone vibration sensors — also lack multistage verification, so natural disturbances are often misread as elephant activity, and a single damaged unit can take the whole alert chain down. E-LeFence's design goal is to close that gap.

Problem Statement

The Department of Wildlife Conservation estimates that about 65% of Sri Lanka's elephant range lies outside national parks and reserves, meaning elephants and humans routinely share the same land. Elephants raid crops and damage property, and people sometimes retaliate — a cycle that has pushed both human and elephant death tolls steadily upward over the past three decades, alongside a sharp rise in the number of properties damaged.

Electric fences reduce this conflict but do not eliminate it, because there is currently no reliable, real-time way to detect when a fence has actually been breached versus simply damaged by a fallen branch, a power outage, or normal wear — or to get that information to the people who need it fast enough to act.

Research Gap

Prior work in this space generally falls into two camps: laser beam systems that use overlapping low-power beams to detect a living object crossing a virtual line, and vibration-based systems that use motion sensors or geophones to pick up the seismic signature of an elephant's footsteps. Both approaches have real limitations:

  • Neither method performs proper multistage verification — a natural disturbance that interrupts every laser beam at once can be misread as an elephant crossing.
  • If an elephant damages the sensor unit itself, the whole detection chain fails silently.
  • Alerting in existing systems is typically limited to SMS or a local siren, with no differentiation between a genuine breach and a fault such as a power outage or a broken wire.

Objectives

The main objective is to build a solution that alerts villagers the moment an elephant breaches the fence, while also making the fence itself easier and cheaper to maintain. This breaks down into four sub-objectives:

  • Detect an approaching elephant and correctly identify it as an elephant, rather than another animal, using an AI model.
  • Independently sense whether voltage is present on the fence wire, to detect faults such as power outages or breaks.
  • Send a warning call out to villagers in the affected area.
  • Notify the relevant authorities when the fence is broken, and distinguish natural or accidental damage from an elephant-caused breach.

Proposed Methodology

E-LeFence is built as a set of cooperating modules, each handling one part of detection, verification, or alerting.

Motion & AI Identification

A PIR sensor detects the presence of a moving object near the fence and triggers the camera unit. Captured images are passed to an onboard CNN-based AI model, which outputs a binary elephant / not-elephant classification. Elephant image data is currently being collected and resized to 64×64 or 96×96 pixels to fit within the ESP32's RAM budget, with the model trained using TensorFlow and periodically re-trained on newly logged field data to improve accuracy over time.

Fence Tilt Monitoring

A gyroscopic sensor on the fence pole tracks its angle. If the tilt changes while the AI model has already flagged an elephant nearby, that combination is treated as a confirmed breach rather than a false alarm from routine fence movement.

Fence Voltage Sensing

The electric fence itself carries a low-current pulse of roughly 2,000–10,000V — far too high to connect directly to a microcontroller. A separate module built around an ACPL-K453 optocoupler IC isolates the sensing circuit electrically, using electromagnetic induction to detect the pulse, rectifying and amplifying the resulting signal, and using it to drive an IR indicator that the microcontroller can safely read. This lets the system tell the difference between a power outage and a physically broken wire.

Night Vision

A separate 950nm IR light module, switchable by the microcontroller, illuminates the scene for the camera at night without disturbing the elephants, since most crossings happen after dark.

Communication & Backend

When the AI model and the gyroscope both trigger at the same time, the device makes an HTTP request to a backend built on Node.js and Express over a 2G cellular connection, using a SIM installed in the module. The backend then places bulk calls to a list of registered phone numbers — alerting either just the authorities, or both authorities and local villagers, depending on the nature of the alert (elephant breach, power outage, or fence damage).

Deterrent (Experimental)

As an optional, still-experimental addition, the team is exploring a bee-sound emitting unit that activates when an elephant approaches, since research shows elephants instinctively avoid the sound of bees — adding a non-harmful deterrent layer alongside the alerting system.

System Block Diagram

The system is split across two microcontrollers working together. An ESP-CAM unit handles sensor input directly — it processes the motion trigger, drives the camera unit, and monitors the fence tilt sensor to determine whether a breakage looks natural or elephant-caused. An ESP32-MAIN unit receives the camera feed, runs the AI model over it, and, once a breach is confirmed, sends the data onward through the GSM/network communication module to the cloud alert system, which places the bulk alert calls. The fence voltage detecting unit feeds directly into this pipeline as well, and the experimental elephant deterrent unit is wired as a downstream output that can be triggered independently.

Testing & Evaluation

Each hardware component will be tested individually, with the AI model tested repetitively against a custom-captured, resized and grayscaled elephant image dataset split 80/20 for training and testing, evaluated using standard classification metrics — accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.

Component Test Objective Testing Method Metric
PIR Sensor Detect motion accurately Test with objects at various distances and sizes Detection range, false triggers
IR Camera with Lighting Ensure clear image capture at night Capture images in different light and weather conditions Image clarity, night visibility
Gyroscope Module Detect fence pole tilt/displacement Simulate various angle changes and observe response Accuracy of angle reading
ESP32 Handle processing load and multitasking Simulate real-time sensor inputs and background processes Response time, system uptime
GSM Module Reliable SMS/call alert transmission Test under weak network and long-range delay Alert success rate, delay time

Because the electric fence pulse (roughly 2,000–10,000V) is impractical to test with directly, the coil-optocoupler circuit is validated using a motorcycle ignition coil as a safe, controlled stand-in — its 10,000–20,000V spark-plug pulse behaves similarly enough to the fence energizer's output to confirm the sensing mechanism before field deployment.

Conclusion

E-LeFence proposes a modular add-on for existing electric fence systems — combining motion detection, low-light imaging, AI-based elephant identification, and voltage-based fence integrity sensing into one device. Built largely from locally available components with AI running at the edge, the goal is a practical, cost-effective way to catch elephant breaches early, warn people in time, and cut down on both human and elephant casualties — with a design that could scale to other regions facing the same conflict.
